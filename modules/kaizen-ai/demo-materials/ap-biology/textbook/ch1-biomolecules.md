# Chapter 1 — The Chemistry of Life: Biomolecules

**Concept:** Biomolecules · **Unit 1**

## Why Water Matters

Life is built on the chemistry of **water**. A water molecule (H₂O) is **polar**: oxygen
pulls shared electrons more strongly than hydrogen, giving oxygen a partial negative
charge and each hydrogen a partial positive charge. These partial charges let water
molecules form **hydrogen bonds** with one another. Although each hydrogen bond is
weak, the sheer number of them gives water its remarkable, life-supporting properties:

- **Cohesion** — water molecules stick to each other, producing surface tension and
  allowing water to be pulled up a plant's xylem in continuous columns.
- **Adhesion** — water sticks to other polar surfaces, aiding capillary action.
- **High specific heat** — hydrogen bonds must be broken to raise temperature, so
  water resists temperature change and stabilizes climates and cells.
- **High heat of vaporization** — evaporative cooling (e.g., sweating) removes large
  amounts of heat.
- **Lower density as a solid** — ice floats, insulating the water below.
- **Universal solvent** — water dissolves polar and charged (hydrophilic) substances,
  forming the medium in which cellular reactions occur.

Substances that mix with water are **hydrophilic**; nonpolar substances that do not are
**hydrophobic**. This simple distinction drives membrane formation and protein folding.

## Carbon and Macromolecules

Living matter is carbon-based. Carbon forms four covalent bonds, allowing large, diverse
**organic molecules**. The four major classes of biological macromolecules are
carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids. Three of these are **polymers** built
from repeating **monomers** joined by **dehydration synthesis** (condensation), which
removes a water molecule to form each bond. The reverse reaction, **hydrolysis**, adds
water to break polymers apart.

### Carbohydrates
Carbohydrates are made of carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen, typically in a 1:2:1 ratio.
**Monosaccharides** (e.g., glucose, C₆H₁₂O₆) are the monomers and serve as immediate
energy sources. Two monosaccharides join by a **glycosidic linkage** to form a
**disaccharide** (e.g., sucrose). **Polysaccharides** are long chains: *starch* and
*glycogen* store energy in plants and animals, while *cellulose* (plants) and *chitin*
(fungi, arthropods) provide structural support. The difference between digestible starch
and indigestible cellulose lies in the orientation of their glycosidic bonds.

### Lipids
Lipids are hydrophobic and are **not** polymers. **Triglycerides (fats)** consist of a
glycerol backbone bonded to three fatty acids. *Saturated* fatty acids have no
carbon-carbon double bonds and pack tightly (solid at room temperature); *unsaturated*
fatty acids have one or more double bonds that kink the chain (liquid oils).
**Phospholipids** have a hydrophilic phosphate "head" and two hydrophobic fatty-acid
"tails"; in water they self-assemble into the **bilayer** that forms all cell membranes.
**Steroids**, such as cholesterol, share a four-ring structure and act in membranes and
as hormone precursors.

### Proteins
Proteins are the workhorses of the cell — enzymes, structural fibers, transporters,
antibodies, and signals. Their monomers are **amino acids**, each with a central carbon
bonded to an amino group, a carboxyl group, a hydrogen, and a variable **R group** (side
chain) that determines the amino acid's chemistry. Amino acids join by **peptide bonds**
(formed by dehydration synthesis) into polypeptides. Protein shape has four levels:

- **Primary structure** — the linear sequence of amino acids.
- **Secondary structure** — local folding into **α-helices** and **β-pleated sheets**,
  stabilized by hydrogen bonds along the backbone.
- **Tertiary structure** — the overall 3-D shape from R-group interactions (hydrogen
  bonds, ionic bonds, disulfide bridges, hydrophobic clustering).
- **Quaternary structure** — two or more polypeptide subunits assembled together
  (e.g., hemoglobin's four chains).

Because function follows shape, high heat or extreme pH can **denature** a protein —
unfolding it and destroying activity.

### Nucleic Acids
Nucleic acids store and transmit genetic information. Their monomers are **nucleotides**,
each made of a five-carbon sugar, a phosphate group, and a nitrogenous base. **DNA**
(deoxyribose sugar; bases A, T, G, C) is a double helix with **antiparallel** strands
held by hydrogen bonds between complementary base pairs (A–T, G–C). **RNA** (ribose
sugar; uracil replaces thymine) is usually single-stranded and carries out gene
expression. Nucleotides link through **phosphodiester bonds** along a sugar-phosphate
backbone.

## Enzymes and Activation Energy

Chemical reactions in cells must occur fast enough to sustain life. **Enzymes** are
biological **catalysts** (usually proteins) that speed reactions by **lowering the
activation energy (Eₐ)** — the energy barrier reactants must cross. Enzymes do not
change whether a reaction is energetically favorable; they change how *fast* it happens.

Each enzyme has an **active site** whose shape is complementary to its **substrate**.
Binding produces an **enzyme-substrate complex**, and the enzyme slightly changes shape
(**induced fit**) to strain bonds and catalyze the reaction, then releases the products
unchanged. Enzyme activity depends on temperature, pH, and the concentrations of enzyme
and substrate, and is tuned by **inhibitors** — *competitive* inhibitors block the
active site, while *noncompetitive* (allosteric) inhibitors bind elsewhere and change the
enzyme's shape. Many pathways use **feedback inhibition**, in which the end product shuts
down an earlier enzyme.

### Labeled Example: Catalase Breaking Down Hydrogen Peroxide
Cells produce toxic hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) as a metabolic byproduct. The enzyme
**catalase** breaks it down:

`2 H₂O₂ → 2 H₂O + O₂`  (catalyzed by catalase)

1. **Substrate binding:** two H₂O₂ molecules enter catalase's active site.
2. **Lowered Eₐ:** catalase stabilizes the transition state, so the reaction proceeds
   without the high heat it would otherwise require.
3. **Product release:** water and oxygen gas are released; the visible bubbling when
   peroxide is placed on a cut is the O₂ produced. The enzyme is unchanged and cycles
   again — a single catalase molecule can process millions of substrate molecules per
   second.

## Check Your Understanding

1. **Why does ice float, and why is this biologically important?**
   *Answer:* As water freezes, hydrogen bonds lock molecules into an open crystal lattice
   that is less dense than liquid water, so ice floats. This insulates the water below,
   allowing aquatic organisms to survive winter.

2. **A mutation changes one amino acid in a protein's primary structure. Explain how this
   could affect the protein's function.**
   *Answer:* Primary structure determines all higher levels of folding. A changed R group
   can alter secondary, tertiary, or quaternary interactions, changing the 3-D shape. If
   the active site or overall conformation changes, the protein may lose (or change)
   function — as in sickle-cell hemoglobin.

3. **How does an enzyme speed up a reaction without being consumed?**
   *Answer:* An enzyme lowers the activation energy by binding substrate in its active
   site and stabilizing the transition state (induced fit). It is released unchanged
   after products form, so it can catalyze the same reaction repeatedly.
